# Example: a meeting that stopped helping

This is a fictional illustration, not a real user's conversation or an evaluated model response.

**User:** Our recurring team meeting keeps getting longer. The handbook says everyone must give an update, but I am not sure the meeting is helping. Think about it from zero, including whether we need it.

**Assistant:** First, let's identify what useful change the meeting is meant to produce. Your answer will help us decide whether to change the updates, the decisions, or the meeting itself.

When does the missing value become noticeable?

- **People discover changes too late.** We would examine how information reaches the people who need it, which may lead to a simpler way to share changes.
- **Work waits for someone to make a decision.** We would examine who can decide and what they need, which may lead to changing the decision process.
- **People do not feel connected to the team.** We would explore what contact feels useful, rather than assuming more updates provide it.
- **Explain this question with an example.** We can compare a concrete situation before choosing where to look.
- **I am not sure yet.** We can start with a recent moment that felt unhelpful.

A mixture or a different explanation is welcome. Choosing one does not commit you to replacing the meeting.

**User:** It is closer to decisions, but I have just realized something: we give updates because we hope someone will notice that we need permission. We rarely ask directly.

**Assistant:** My tentative understanding is that updates are doing the work of an unclear request for a decision. We should check that against a recent example. Is the intended result that a person knows which decision they need and can get it from the right person without waiting for the next meeting?

**User:** Yes. People already have decision owners. They just do not know how to raise the request. We can change the meeting and its format, but keep those responsibilities.

**Assistant:** Starting with no existing meeting, the simplest arrangement is a clear decision request sent to the existing owner: what needs deciding, what is blocked, the relevant facts, and the date by which the answer matters. The owner responds or identifies the missing information. Everyone affected can see the decision afterward. A conversation is arranged when discussion is needed.

Compared with the current meeting, the possible futures are:

| Approach | What people would experience | Burden or uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep the meeting and add explicit decision requests | People can ask directly in a familiar setting | Requests still wait for the meeting, and update preparation remains |
| Send requests to the decision owner as needed | People can seek a decision when work becomes blocked | Requests must be clear; owners need a reliable way to notice and answer them |
| Stop the meeting and use existing conversations | Preparation falls away | This only works if decisions remain visible and requests actually reach their owners |

The second approach fits the stated intention, but its response reliability is still a hypothesis. Try it on a real blocked decision and check whether the owner could answer, whether anything important was missing, and whether the people affected understood the result. Then decide what recurring contact, if any, still earns its place.

Useful information from past updates can be retained. The handbook's update round is a previous design choice whose usefulness can now be reassessed.
